## Definition

An internal summary of how a brand wants customers to think about it. In the form that Alice Tybout gives in *Kellogg on Branding*, the statement has four parts:

- Target: the customers that the brand serves, often with an insight into the goal that brings them to the category.
- Frame of reference: the goal that the brand serves, usually stated as the category that it belongs to.
- Point of difference: the benefit that makes the brand better than the other choices in the frame.
- Reason to believe: the proof of that benefit. It is an attribute of the product, or an image of the people and the occasions of its use.

The team uses the statement to align each decision that customers see, such as the design, the touchpoints, and the advertising. Customers do not read it.

## Notes

April Dunford calls the components of positioning, in essence, the blanks of a positioning statement. Her target customers fill the target, her market category fills the frame of reference, her value fills the point of difference, and her unique attributes give the reason to believe. Write the statement after that work, not in place of it, as the positioning practice area of product strategy states.

A brand must share the key features of a category to claim a place in it. These shared features are the points of parity. A new product that the company calls a beer, but that has no alcohol, is not credible.

Tybout gives two tests. People outside the company read the statement and say where they would use the brand, why it is better, and whether they believe it. If they cannot say, the statement lacks clarity. If they do not believe it, it lacks credibility. Then the team puts the name of a competitor in place of the brand name. If the statement still sounds right, it lacks distinctiveness.

A reason to believe can weaken another claim. Tybout's example is a light beer: fewer calories support "less filling" but cast doubt on "great taste" if customers think the two go against each other.
