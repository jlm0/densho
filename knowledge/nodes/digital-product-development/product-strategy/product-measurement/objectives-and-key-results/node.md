## Definition

A goal-setting system from Andy Grove at Intel, which John Doerr brought to Google. An objective states what to achieve. Key results state how the team will know that it got there: measurable, time-bound milestones that a reader can verify. Teams set OKRs each quarter and keep them apart from pay. Google's playbook separates committed OKRs, which the team expects to meet in full, from aspirational OKRs, which aim beyond what the team knows how to reach. Each key result gets a score from `0.0` to `1.0` at the end of the cycle. A committed OKR expects `1.0`. An aspirational OKR expects `0.7` on average.

## Notes

A key result that names an activity, such as "consult", "help", "analyze", or "participate", describes work, not its effect. Google's example is "publish average and tail latency measurements from six cells by March 7" in place of "assess latency". A key result in team-internal terms, such as "launch version 4.1", also falls short: "improve sign-ups by 25 percent" states the effect. A postmortem on a missed commitment is not a punishment. It helps the team plan the next commitment.
